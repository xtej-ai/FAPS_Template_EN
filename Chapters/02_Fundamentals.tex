\chapter[Fundamentals]{Fundamentals of Automated Driving Safety and Safety Assurance}
\label{ch:fundamentals}

This chapter establishes the terminology and the stable, established knowledge on
which the remainder of the thesis builds. It first describes automated driving
systems and their operating envelope, then the two safety paradigms that frame the
work, followed by safety assurance cases and the notation used to express them, the
definition of a safety performance indicator, the surrogate safety metrics that serve
as candidate signals, the simulation environment used for evaluation, and the
statistical concepts needed to evaluate rare events. The current state of research
that builds on these foundations is reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:sota}.

\section{Automated Driving Systems and the Operational Design Domain}
\label{sec:fund_ads}

An \ac{ADS} is the combination of hardware and software that performs the entire
dynamic driving task on a sustained basis. The taxonomy of SAE~J3016 distinguishes
six levels of driving automation, from no automation at Level~0 to full automation at
Level~5, and reserves the term \ac{ADS} for the systems at Levels~3 to~5 that perform
the dynamic driving task without the constant supervision of a human driver
\cite{saej3016}. The dynamic driving task is commonly decomposed into the functions of
perception, prediction, planning, and control, together with a function that monitors
the operating conditions. This functional decomposition is used throughout the thesis
because it names the tasks that any driving system must perform, independently of the
particular software architecture that performs them.

The \ac{DDT} comprises the sustained lateral and longitudinal control of the vehicle
together with the monitoring of the driving environment and the response to objects
and events within it \cite{saej3016}. The distinction between the levels therefore
lies in who performs which part of this task and who acts when the system can no
longer perform it. Up to Level~2 a human driver monitors the environment at all times;
from Level~3 onwards the system monitors it, and from Level~4 onwards the system also
performs the fallback itself by bringing the vehicle to a minimal risk condition
\cite{saej3016}. For the assurance of a Level~4 system this has a direct consequence:
no human is available to notice that a margin is eroding, so whatever observes the
erosion has to be part of the technical system or of the process that operates it.

The four functions named above act in sequence on every cycle of the driving task.
Perception builds a representation of the surrounding objects from sensor data,
prediction estimates how those objects will move, planning selects a trajectory that
the vehicle is to follow, and control turns that trajectory into steering, throttle
and brake commands. A deficiency in an early function propagates into the behaviour of
the vehicle only through the later ones, so it becomes visible in the motion of the
vehicle relative to other road users only after a delay. This propagation is the
reason why the indicators of this thesis distinguish between signals taken from the
behaviour of the vehicle and signals taken from inside a function, a distinction that
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} formalises as two indicator tiers.

Every \ac{ADS} is specified to operate only within a defined \ac{ODD}, that is, the
set of operating conditions under which it is designed to function, including
environmental, geographical, and time-of-day constraints as well as the presence or
absence of certain traffic and road features \cite{saej3016}. The \ac{ODD} is central
to safety assurance, because a claim of safety holds only within the declared
conditions, and a departure from those conditions is itself a hazard. Thresholds on
the indicators derived in this thesis are therefore conditioned on the \ac{ODD}, so
that the value considered acceptable under one set of conditions, for example dry
weather, differs from the value considered acceptable under another, for example rain.

The \ac{ODD} also bounds what any evidence about the system can establish. A claim
supported by data gathered under one set of conditions transfers to another set only
when the two are shown to be comparable, and the assurance guidance for autonomous
systems in complex environments accordingly splits the top-level safety claim into a
claim about operation inside the defined operating context and a separate claim about
operation outside it \cite{hawkins2022sace}. The same split applies to an indicator:
its metric threshold carries meaning only for the population of operating conditions
from which it was calibrated, which is why the transferability of thresholds between
configurations is tested explicitly in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Functional Safety and the Safety of the Intended Functionality}
\label{sec:fund_safety}

Two complementary standards frame the safety of an \ac{ADS}. ISO~26262 addresses
functional safety, defined as the absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards caused
by malfunctioning behaviour of electrical and electronic systems, and provides a
risk-based development process organised around automotive safety integrity levels
\cite{iso26262}. Its scope is the fault, that is, a component that fails to behave as
specified. ISO~21448 addresses the \ac{SOTIF}, defined as the absence of unreasonable
risk due to hazards resulting from functional insufficiencies of the intended
functionality or from reasonably foreseeable misuse, when no component has failed
\cite{iso21448}. Its scope is the situation in which every component operates as
specified yet the specification itself is inadequate, which is characteristic of the
perception and machine-learning elements of an \ac{ADS}.

ISO~26262 derives its requirements from a hazard analysis and risk assessment. Each
hazardous event is rated for the severity of its possible harm, the exposure to the
operational situation in which it can occur, and the controllability of the situation
by the persons involved, and the combination of the three ratings determines an
\ac{ASIL} from A, the least demanding, to D, the most demanding, or a rating of
quality management where no safety requirement is needed \cite{iso26262}. The
\ac{ASIL} then fixes the rigour of the development and verification measures applied
to the safety goals and to the requirements derived from them. The scheme presumes
that a hazard can be traced to a malfunction, and it measures risk through
categories that are assigned before operation rather than observed during it.

ISO~21448 addresses the hazards that this scheme leaves out. A \ac{SOTIF}-related
hazard requires two conditions to coincide: a triggering condition in the
environment, such as extreme weather, an unusual road layout or an unexpected
manoeuvre of another road user, and a performance limitation of the system in
sensing, perception, decision-making or execution, or a deficiency of its
specification \cite{iso21448} \cite{wang2024sotif}. Neither condition is a fault in
the sense of ISO~26262. A perception function can operate exactly as trained and
still misjudge a situation that its training did not cover, and a planner can follow
its specification exactly when that specification omits a case.

ISO~21448 organises the scenarios of a system into four areas, according to whether a
scenario is known or unknown to the developers and whether the behaviour of the system
in it is safe or unsafe \cite{iso21448}. Table~\ref{tab:sotif_areas} sets out the four
areas together with the activity that addresses each. The objective of the standard is
to shrink the known unsafe area through design measures and the unknown unsafe area
through verification and validation until the residual risk is acceptable. The unknown
unsafe area, however, cannot be shown to be empty by any finite programme of analysis
or testing before release, because a scenario that has not been imagined cannot be
analysed and a scenario that has not been met cannot be tested.

The residual risk that remains after development is consequently carried into
operation. Analyses, improvements and verification during development cannot remove
it entirely, and the operation phase has to observe whether triggering conditions and
the internal state of the system combine into hazardous behaviour that development
did not anticipate \cite{wang2024sotif}. Wang et al. also identify the definition of
acceptance criteria as an open problem and list the comparison with traffic
statistics, the comparison with experienced and cautious human drivers, and
risk-acceptance principles such as GAMAB and ALARP as candidate bases
\cite{wang2024sotif}. Both points recur in this thesis: the first motivates indicators
that are monitored after release, and the second marks the boundary of what the
statistical thresholds of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} can claim.

The distinction matters for the present work because the two paradigms call for
different reasoning. A fault-based hazard is well captured by the failure logic of
classical reliability analysis, whereas a functional insufficiency is not, since there
is no failed component to point to. The indicators derived in this thesis are intended
to observe the developing consequences of both kinds of hazard in the behaviour of the
vehicle, which is why the derivation addresses functional impossibilities rather than
component failures.

A third standard sets the frame for monitoring after release. Whereas ISO~26262 and
ISO~21448 prescribe what is to be done during development, ANSI/UL~4600 is
goal-based: it requires a safety case for a fully autonomous product and leaves the
means of supporting it to the developer, while requiring that the case be kept valid
over the lifecycle through monitored indicators \cite{ul4600} \cite{wang2024sotif}.
This combination of a goal-based safety case with field monitoring is the setting in
which the indicators of this thesis are to be used.

\section{Safety Assurance Cases and the Goal Structuring Notation}
\label{sec:fund_gsn}

A \ac{SAC} is a structured argument, supported by a body of evidence, that a system is
acceptably safe to operate in a defined context \cite{bishop2000methodology}. It makes
the reasoning from evidence to a top-level safety claim explicit and reviewable, rather
than leaving it implicit in a collection of documents. Kelly and Weaver require of such
a case that it communicate a clear, comprehensive and defensible argument, and they
identify three principal elements: the safety requirements and objectives, the safety
argument, and the safety evidence \cite{kelly2004gsn}. Argument and evidence depend on
each other. An argument without evidence is unfounded and therefore unconvincing,
whereas evidence without an argument leaves unexplained how it satisfies the
objectives \cite{kelly2004gsn}.

Bishop and Bloomfield describe the same structure in terms of claims, arguments and
evidence, with an inference rule connecting evidence to the claim it supports
\cite{bishop2000methodology}. They distinguish three types of argument, namely
deterministic arguments that apply predetermined rules, probabilistic arguments that
establish a numerical level through statistical reasoning, and qualitative arguments
that rest on compliance with rules only indirectly linked to the property claimed
\cite{bishop2000methodology}. They also name the sources from which evidence can be
drawn: the design, the development process, simulated experience, and prior field
experience. Two further points from their methodology are used later in this thesis.
Evidence may itself be a sub-claim supported by a subsidiary argument, and a claim
supported by two independent arguments tolerates a flaw in either of them
\cite{bishop2000methodology}.

The \ac{GSN} is a graphical notation for expressing such arguments and is widely used
in safety-critical domains \cite{kelly2004gsn}. Its community standard defines the core
element types used in this thesis \cite{gsncs2021}, which are summarised in
Figure~\ref{fig:gsn_notation}: a goal states a claim to be established, a strategy
explains how a goal is decomposed into sub-goals, a solution provides the evidence that
supports a goal, and context, assumption, and justification elements record the
conditions under which the argument holds. An argument is built by decomposing the
top-level goal through strategies into sub-goals until each remaining goal can be
discharged by a solution.

    goal, strategy, solution, context, assumption, and justification, together with

    \label{fig:gsn_notation}
\end{figure}

Two relations connect the elements. The supported-by relation links a goal to the
strategies, sub-goals and solutions from which it is inferred, and the in-context-of
relation links a goal or strategy to the context, assumption and justification
elements that qualify it \cite{gsncs2021}. A goal that has not yet been decomposed
further is marked as undeveloped, which makes the incompleteness of an argument
visible rather than hiding it. The notation records the structure of an argument but
not its strength: a goal structure is inductive rather than deductive, because the
truth of all solutions makes the top-level goal probable rather than certain, and the
confidence that the child elements warrant in their parent is a judgement that the
notation by itself leaves implicit \cite{kelly2004gsn}.

Several extensions make the notation manageable for large systems. Safety case
patterns capture recurring argument structures, documented with their intent,
motivation, structure and applicability, so that a successful argument can be reused
without carrying over assumptions that do not hold in the new context
\cite{kelly1998patterns}. Patterns use parameterised expressions, multiplicity and
choice to describe a family of arguments rather than a single one, and these
constructs have been given a formal definition as a step towards an assurance case
language \cite{matsuno2014gsnpatterns}. Modular safety cases partition an argument
along the architecture of the system, so that the argument for one module can be
developed and assessed separately and referenced from another module
\cite{kelly2004gsn} \cite{gsncs2021}. Finally, the maintenance of an argument through
the operational life of a system is recognised as a problem of its own, because
changing requirements, new evidence and design changes can each challenge a goal
that was considered supported at approval \cite{kelly2004gsn}.

Guidance for autonomous systems builds directly on these extensions. The \ac{SACE}
guidance of the University of York provides a set of argument patterns organised in
eight stages, from the assurance of the operating context to the assurance of
verification, and it states its top-level claim as the system being sufficiently safe
throughout its operational life \cite{hawkins2022sace}. It marks the points at which a
confidence argument is required with assurance claim points, and it requires that a
runtime monitor be independent of the components it monitors, noting that trends in
monitored behaviour can act as a leading indicator of future unsafe behaviour
\cite{hawkins2022sace}. For automated vehicles, Loba et al.\ derive structural
requirements for a \ac{GSN}-based argumentation and propose a generic structure that
separates a product argument, a process argument, a conformance argument, a context
argument and a soundness argument, with explicit concern for how valid the
assumptions of the argument remain during field operation \cite{loba2025harmonized}.

The \ac{GSN} is adopted here for two reasons. First, its decomposition of a top-level
goal into sub-goals produces the claim hierarchy to which safety performance indicators
are attached in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. Second, a goal that is expressed as a
bare assertion can be turned into a monitorable claim by attaching an indicator to it,
which is the mechanism by which the assurance case is connected to operational data.
In the terms of Bishop and Bloomfield, such an indicator supplies a probabilistic
argument from simulated or field experience to a goal that development evidence alone
cannot discharge. The assurance cases of this thesis are modelled in a model-based
safety-engineering environment that supports the construction of \ac{GSN} arguments
and their linkage to other safety artefacts.

\section{Safety Performance Indicators}
\label{sec:fund_spi}

A \ac{SPI} is a metric, supported by evidence, that uses a threshold comparison to
condition a claim within a safety assurance case; it is a metric-threshold pair that
measures a specific aspect of safety \cite{laxman2024}. Three parts therefore make up
every \ac{SPI}, and all three are needed. The first is the metric, a quantity computed
from data, such as a count, a rate, or a physical measurement. The second is the
threshold, a value that separates acceptable from unacceptable, against which the metric
is compared. The third is the claim, the specific statement in the assurance case that
the comparison is taken to support or to challenge. A metric on its own does not
constitute an \ac{SPI}, because the same numerical value can be acceptable in one
operating context and unacceptable in another; only when the metric is paired with a
threshold and tied to a claim does it carry a safety meaning
\cite{laxman2024, koopman2022howsafe}.

Metrics may be quantitative or qualitative. A quantitative metric is a measured or
counted value, for example a rate of events per distance driven, whereas a qualitative
metric records the presence or the completeness of a process step, for example whether
a required review has been carried out. Because context governs acceptability, the
threshold is frequently conditioned on the operating conditions, so that the value
regarded as acceptable in one part of the \ac{ODD} differs from that in another. The
threshold itself has two distinct roles that are developed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: a per-event threshold defines what counts as a single
violation, and a rate-level threshold defines how often such violations may occur
before the violation rate itself is anomalous.

The property that matters most for this thesis is whether an indicator is leading or
lagging, according to whether it is observed before or after a loss event. A lagging
\ac{SPI} reports an outcome after the fact; the collision rate, the injury rate, and
the count of automation disengagements are lagging indicators of an \ac{ADS}. A leading
\ac{SPI} observes a condition that precedes a loss event and therefore offers the
opportunity to act before it occurs; the rate at which the time-to-collision falls
below a set value, the rate of activation of automatic emergency braking, and the rate
at which a following gap drops below a safe distance are leading indicators
\cite{koopman2022howsafe}. An effective programme combines both, but only leading
indicators enable a proactive response, which is why their systematic construction is
the central concern of this work.

The framework for measuring automated vehicle safety by Fraade-Blanar et al.\ gives
the same distinction a precise footing. Lagging measures are observations of safety
outcomes involving harm, such as crashes, injuries and deaths, whereas leading
measures reflect performance, activity and prevention and act as proxies for the
lagging ones; in the terms of the Haddon matrix they are drawn from the pre-crash
phase \cite{fraadeblanar2018}. Because the events counted by a leading measure occur
more often than those counted by a lagging measure, a leading measure reaches
statistical confidence at a lower cumulative exposure, which allows the safety of a
system to be judged earlier in development and deployment \cite{fraadeblanar2018}.

The same framework states the properties that any such measure has to satisfy and the
ways in which a leading measure can fail. A measure has to be valid, feasible,
reliable and non-manipulatable \cite{fraadeblanar2018}. The link between a proxy and
the outcome it stands for is required not to depend on the circumstances under
which either occurs, for example being strong at high speed and weak at low speed. Disengagements
illustrate how a measure can fail these properties: they are sensitive to the training
and risk tolerance of the safety driver, they presuppose a safety driver, and their
use as a target creates an incentive to disengage as rarely as possible
\cite{fraadeblanar2018}. For measures of driving behaviour the framework asks that they
be objective, physics-based, available with current technology, and able to separate
the road user who initiates an unsafe situation from the one who responds to it, so
that evasive action is not penalised \cite{fraadeblanar2018}. These properties serve
as criteria for the signals selected in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, and the last of
them reappears in the definition of a pedestrian collision in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiment}.

Every indicator is stated against a unit of exposure. A violation count has a meaning
only relative to the amount of operation in which it was gathered, whether that is
measured in distance driven, operating hours or manoeuvres performed, and a comparison
between configurations is meaningful only when it is made per unit of the same
exposure \cite{fraadeblanar2018}. For a manoeuvre-specific use case such as the one
studied here, the manoeuvre is the natural unit, because the conflicts of interest
arise once per manoeuvre rather than continuously along a route.

A concrete example makes the structure explicit. Consider the assurance-case goal that
the vehicle keeps a safe separation from a leading vehicle. A candidate leading \ac{SPI}
for this goal pairs the time-to-collision metric of Section~\ref{sec:fund_metrics} with
a per-event threshold $\theta$ and a rate-level threshold $T$, and conditions the goal
in the form given in Equation~\ref{eq:spi_example}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{count}\!\left(\mathrm{TTC}_{\min} < \theta\right)\ \mathrm{per}\ N\ \mathrm{manoeuvres}\ <\ T
    \label{eq:spi_example}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Example leading safety performance indicator on time-to-collision}

where $\mathrm{TTC}_{\min}$ is the minimum time-to-collision observed during a
manoeuvre, $\theta$ is the per-event threshold below which the manoeuvre counts as a
violation, $N$ is a fixed number of manoeuvres over which the count is taken, and $T$
is the rate-level threshold. A measured violation rate at or above $T$ challenges the
safe-separation goal and, within an operational safety assurance process, triggers a
review of that part of the assurance case rather than waiting for a collision to be
recorded. The concept, its origin in the aviation safety management systems of the
International Civil Aviation Organization \cite{icao9859}, and its requirement for
automated driving by ANSI/UL~4600 \cite{ul4600} and BSI PAS~1881 \cite{pas1881} are all
introduced here as fundamentals; how a leading \ac{SPI} such as this one is derived
systematically from a claim is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Surrogate Safety Metrics}
\label{sec:fund_metrics}

Because collisions are rare, safety is commonly assessed through surrogate metrics that
quantify how close an interaction comes to a collision, and these metrics supply the
signal component of the indicators derived later. The most established is the
\ac{TTC}, introduced by Hayward, which expresses the time remaining before two road
users on a collision course would collide if their velocities remained unchanged
\cite{hayward1972ttc}. For a following vehicle approaching a leading vehicle along the
same path, the \ac{TTC} is given by Equation~\ref{eq:ttc}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TTC} = \frac{d_\mathrm{rel}}{v_\mathrm{rel}}, \qquad v_\mathrm{rel} > 0
    \label{eq:ttc}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Time-to-collision}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $d_\mathrm{rel}$ \> relative distance between the two road users in m \\
    $v_\mathrm{rel}$ \> relative closing speed, positive when the gap is closing, in m/s
\end{tabbing}

Equation~\ref{eq:ttc} presumes that both road users move along one line. When they
move in a plane, as at an intersection, the same idea is expressed along the line of
sight between them: the range is the distance between the two reference points, the
range rate is its derivative, and the \ac{TTC} is the range divided by the negative
range rate whenever the range is decreasing, as given in Equation~\ref{eq:ttc_los}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TTC}_\mathrm{LOS} = -\frac{r}{\dot{r}}, \qquad
    \dot{r} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf{p} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{v}}{r}, \qquad \dot{r} < 0
    \label{eq:ttc_los}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Line-of-sight time-to-collision}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $\Delta\mathbf{p}$ \> position of the other road user relative to the ego vehicle in m \\
    $\Delta\mathbf{v}$ \> velocity of the other road user relative to the ego vehicle in m/s \\
    $r$ \> range $\lvert \Delta\mathbf{p} \rvert$ between the two reference points in m \\
    $\dot{r}$ \> range rate, negative when the range is decreasing, in m/s
\end{tabbing}

The line-of-sight formulation inherits the central limitation of every \ac{TTC}: it
extrapolates the current relative velocity unchanged. It responds only to the
component of the relative motion that lies along the line of sight, so two road users
whose paths cross at an angle produce a large value while they are still far apart and
a rapidly falling value once their paths almost meet. It also takes no account of
whether the extrapolated paths actually intersect, so a road user that closes in range
but passes at a safe lateral distance still produces a finite value.

The second of these limitations is removed by the closest point of approach, which
asks not when the range reaches zero but when it is smallest and how small it becomes.
Under the same constant-velocity assumption, the time until the \ac{CPA} and the
distance at that moment follow from the relative position and velocity as given in
Equation~\ref{eq:cpa}.

\begin{equation}
    t_\mathrm{CPA} = -\frac{\Delta\mathbf{p} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{v}}{\lvert \Delta\mathbf{v} \rvert^{2}},
    \qquad
    d_\mathrm{CPA} = \left\lvert \Delta\mathbf{p} + \Delta\mathbf{v}\, t_\mathrm{CPA} \right\rvert
    \label{eq:cpa}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Time and distance at the closest point of approach}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $t_\mathrm{CPA}$ \> time until the closest point of approach in s \\
    $d_\mathrm{CPA}$ \> distance between the two road users at that time in m \\
    $\Delta\mathbf{p}$, $\Delta\mathbf{v}$ \> relative position in m and relative velocity in m/s, as in
    Equation~\ref{eq:ttc_los}
\end{tabbing}

A positive $t_\mathrm{CPA}$ together with a small $d_\mathrm{CPA}$ describes a road
user who will pass close to the vehicle soon, whereas a large $d_\mathrm{CPA}$
identifies a road user who will miss the vehicle however short the time. Gating the
time on the distance therefore separates conflicts from mere proximity, which is the
property required for a crossing pedestrian whose walking line may or may not
intersect the path of the vehicle. The pedestrian indicator PED\_CPA of this thesis is
built on this quantity.

A related time-based measure is the \ac{PET}, introduced by Allen et al., which
applies to crossing conflicts and measures the time between the moment the first road
user leaves a shared conflict area and the moment the second enters it
\cite{allen1978pet}. A small \ac{PET} indicates that two road users occupied nearly
the same space at nearly the same time. Unlike the \ac{TTC} and the closest point of
approach, the \ac{PET} is known only once both road users have passed the conflict
area, so it describes how close an encounter came rather than how close it is about
to come. Where a required response is of interest rather than the time available, the
required deceleration to avoid a collision, given in Equation~\ref{eq:areq}, expresses
how hard the vehicle has to brake, and a value approaching the physical limit
of the vehicle indicates a critical situation.

\begin{equation}
    a_\mathrm{req} = \frac{v_\mathrm{rel}^{2}}{2\,d_\mathrm{rel}}
    \label{eq:areq}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Required deceleration to avoid a collision}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $a_\mathrm{req}$ \> required deceleration in m/s\textsuperscript{2} \\
    $v_\mathrm{rel}$ \> relative closing speed in m/s \\
    $d_\mathrm{rel}$ \> relative distance in m
\end{tabbing}

Equation~\ref{eq:areq} treats the obstacle as stationary at the end of the available
distance. It therefore states the braking needed to stop short of a point, not the
braking needed to avoid a road user who is itself moving,
and it grows without bound as the distance approaches zero. Both properties matter for
an implementation, because a moving partner makes the true demand different from the
computed one and a very short distance makes the value numerically unstable.

For the following conflict the time headway is the established complement to the
\ac{TTC}. It is the time a following vehicle needs to reach the current position of
the vehicle ahead at its own current speed, as given in Equation~\ref{eq:thw} \cite{westhofen2023}, and it
remains defined when the two vehicles travel at the same speed, where the \ac{TTC} is
undefined.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{THW} = \frac{d_\mathrm{gap}}{v_\mathrm{f}}, \qquad v_\mathrm{f} > 0
    \label{eq:thw}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Time headway}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $\mathrm{THW}$ \> time headway in s \\
    $d_\mathrm{gap}$ \> distance between the rear of the vehicle ahead and the front of the
    following vehicle in m \\
    $v_\mathrm{f}$ \> speed of the following vehicle in m/s
\end{tabbing}

A formal alternative defines a safe distance from explicit assumptions about
behaviour. The Responsibility-Sensitive Safety model of Shalev-Shwartz et al. defines
the minimum safe longitudinal distance behind a leading vehicle from the response time
and the assumed acceleration and braking capabilities, as given in
Equation~\ref{eq:rss} \cite{shalev2017rss}. A distance below this value denotes a state
that the model regards as unsafe, which makes the model a natural source of a
distance-based threshold.

\begin{equation}
    d_\mathrm{min} = \left[ v_r \rho + \frac{1}{2} a_\mathrm{max}^\mathrm{acc}\,\rho^{2}
    + \frac{\left(v_r + \rho\,a_\mathrm{max}^\mathrm{acc}\right)^{2}}{2\,a_\mathrm{min}^\mathrm{brake}}
    - \frac{v_f^{2}}{2\,a_\mathrm{max}^\mathrm{brake}} \right]_{+}
    \label{eq:rss}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Responsibility-Sensitive Safety minimum safe longitudinal distance}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $v_r,\ v_f$ \> speed of the rear and the front vehicle in m/s \\
    $\rho$ \> response time of the rear vehicle in s \\
    $a_\mathrm{max}^\mathrm{acc}$ \> maximum acceleration during the response time in m/s\textsuperscript{2} \\
    $a_\mathrm{min}^\mathrm{brake}$ \> minimum braking of the rear vehicle in m/s\textsuperscript{2} \\
    $a_\mathrm{max}^\mathrm{brake}$ \> maximum braking of the front vehicle in m/s\textsuperscript{2} \\
    $[\,\cdot\,]_{+}$ \> the positive part, that is $\max(\cdot,0)$
\end{tabbing}

The Responsibility-Sensitive Safety distance differs in kind from the other measures.
The \ac{TTC}, the closest point of approach, the \ac{PET} and the required
deceleration describe what is happening between two road users, whereas the
Responsibility-Sensitive Safety distance prescribes what ought to be maintained under
stated assumptions about their capabilities \cite{shalev2017rss}. Its value as a
threshold therefore depends entirely on parameters such as the response time and the
braking capabilities, which have to be fixed for every vehicle and every road
condition before the distance can be computed.

quantity each returns, and Table~\ref{tab:surrogate_metrics} summarises these
differences, since the suitability of a signal for a given conflict is what governs its
selection in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

Every surrogate metric is evaluated over time, whereas an indicator decides once per
manoeuvre. A metric sampled at every simulation step yields a time series, and the
indicator reduces it to a single value per manoeuvre, its minimum for a metric whose
critical tail is lower and its maximum for a metric whose critical tail is upper. The
reduction keeps the most critical moment of the manoeuvre and discards the rest,
which makes the per-manoeuvre values independent observations even though the steps
within one manoeuvre are strongly correlated. The same reduction is used for the
calibration of every metric threshold in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Simulation-Based Safety Assessment}
\label{sec:fund_sim}

Because field data on rare safety-critical events is sparse and reporting is
inconsistent, safety-critical behaviour is studied in simulation, where scenarios can
be generated in large numbers and with controlled parameters. CARLA is an open-source
simulator for automated driving research that provides sensor models, traffic
participants, and configurable environmental conditions, together with a programmable
interface for scripting scenarios and logging ground-truth state \cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}.
In this thesis it is used to generate the scenarios from which the derived indicators
are evaluated.

CARLA follows a client-server architecture in which the server runs the simulation and
a client retrieves data and requests changes to the world \cite{carladocs_sync}. By
default the server advances as fast as it can without waiting for the client, and the
simulated time between two steps varies with the computational load. In synchronous
mode the server instead waits for a tick from the client before it advances, and with
a fixed time-step every step covers the same simulated interval; a step of 0.05\,s,
for example, gives 20 steps per simulated second \cite{carladocs_sync}. Physics
substepping divides each step further, and the documentation states that the physics
cannot run properly with a time-step larger than 0.1\,s \cite{carladocs_sync}.
Deterministic repetition of a run requires synchronous mode, a fixed time-step, a
reloaded world for every repetition, and commands that are applied in batches
\cite{carladocs_sync}. These conditions are what allow every run of this thesis to be
regenerated from its seed.

Road users other than the ego vehicle are animated by built-in controllers. Vehicles
in autopilot mode are driven by the Traffic Manager, a client-side module that passes
every vehicle through a sequence of stages for localisation along a waypoint path,
collision checking against nearby vehicles, handling of traffic lights and junction
priority, and motion planning, where a proportional-integral-derivative controller
converts the planned targets into throttle, brake and steering commands
\cite{carladocs_tm}. Its behaviour can be adjusted for each vehicle, for example to
ignore traffic lights, other vehicles or walkers, to keep a given distance to the
vehicle ahead, or to drive at a given fraction of the speed limit, and its
deterministic mode requires synchronous operation and a seed that is set again after
every world reload \cite{carladocs_tm}. These per-vehicle parameters are what a
scenario generator varies to produce conflicts of different severity.

Contacts are recorded by a collision sensor attached to an actor. The sensor registers
an event whenever its parent actor collides with anything in the world and reports the
frame, the simulation time, the other actor involved and the normal impulse of the
contact, producing one event per collision and frame \cite{carladocs_sensors}. Because
the other actor is reported with its identity, every contact can be attributed to the
road user involved, which is the basis on which collisions are assigned to conflict
chains in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiment}.

A simulator answers questions about a model of the world rather than about the world.
Its vehicle dynamics, the behaviour models of other road users and, where sensors are
simulated, the sensor models all differ from their real counterparts, so a quantity
measured in simulation transfers to the field only as far as those models are
faithful. The use of ground-truth state has a further consequence for the scope of
the evaluation, which is discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, because signals
that depend on the internal outputs of a perception module cannot be observed when the
ego vehicle is driven by a rule-based agent on ground truth.

Simulation does not by itself remove the rarity of safety-critical events. Feng et al.\
observe that, owing to the high dimensionality of the driving environment and the
rareness of safety-critical events, testing in a naturalistic driving environment requires
hundreds of millions of miles to demonstrate the safety performance of an
automated vehicle, even in simulation \cite{feng2021nade}. They show that sparse
adversarial adjustments of the naturalistic environment reduce the required test
distance by orders of magnitude without biasing the evaluation, provided that the
adjustments are learned and the resulting estimates are corrected by importance
sampling \cite{feng2021nade}. The corollary matters for this thesis: a campaign that
provokes conflicts without such a correction produces many collisions quickly, but its
collision frequency is not an estimate of the frequency in naturalistic operation.

\section{Statistical Basis for Evaluating Rare Events}
\label{sec:fund_statistics}

Every result of this thesis is a count of runs in which an event occurred, and most of
the events of interest are rare. The statistical treatment of such counts is
therefore part of the foundation of the evaluation, and three of its elements recur
throughout: the estimation of a proportion with an interval, the comparison of two
proportions through their ratio, and the treatment of a count of zero.

A proportion estimated from $k$ events in $n$ trials is uncertain, and the uncertainty
is largest relative to the estimate when $k$ is small. The common normal-approximation
interval centres on $k/n$ and collapses to a single point when $k$ is zero, which
claims certainty precisely where the data are weakest. The score interval of Wilson
avoids this by inverting the score test rather than the estimate, and it remains
informative for counts of zero and for counts close to $n$
\cite{wilson1927probable}. It is given in Equation~\ref{eq:wilson}.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\hat{p} + \dfrac{z^{2}}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}\,(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \dfrac{z^{2}}{4n^{2}}}}
    {1 + \dfrac{z^{2}}{n}}
    \label{eq:wilson}
\end{equation}
\equationentry{Wilson score interval of a proportion}

where:
\begin{tabbing}
    \hspace{2.2cm} \= \kill
    $\hat{p}$ \> observed proportion $k/n$, dimensionless \\
    $n$ \> number of trials, here eligible runs, dimensionless \\
    $z$ \> standard normal quantile, 1.96 for a two-sided 95\,\% interval, dimensionless
\end{tabbing}

For $k = 0$ the lower limit of Equation~\ref{eq:wilson} is zero and the upper limit
is positive, so a campaign without any collision still yields an upper bound on the
collision probability per run. This bound shrinks only as $n$ grows, which is the
statistical form of the argument of Kalra and Paddock that the absence of failures
demonstrates a low failure rate only after an exposure that is large relative to the
rate to be demonstrated \cite{kalra2016driving}.

A leading indicator is useful only if a violation changes the probability of the loss
that it anticipates. The natural measure of that change is the relative risk, the
ratio of the probability of the loss among runs with a violation to the probability
among runs without one. It is the measure used in cohort studies, where a group
exposed to a factor is compared with an unexposed group, and its logarithm is
approximately normally distributed, which gives the interval of Katz et al.\
\cite{katz1978risk}. A relative risk of one means that a violation carries no
information about the loss, and an interval that excludes one means that the
association is unlikely to arise by chance.

The relative risk and the interval of Katz et al.\ are undefined when one cell of the
underlying two-by-two classification is zero, which occurs whenever every loss is
preceded by a violation or no loss occurs at all. A count of zero in such a table is
not evidence of an infinite or a zero ratio but of a sample too small to resolve it,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:experiment} states how such cells are treated and how the
resulting value is reported. The three elements together determine which statements
the evaluation can support: an interval for every proportion, a ratio with an interval
for every association, and an explicit bound wherever a count is zero.
